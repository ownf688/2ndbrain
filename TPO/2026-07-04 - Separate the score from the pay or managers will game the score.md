# Separate the score from the pay or managers will game the score

> **The take:** If a manager knows a "4" means a 10% raise, they'll score for the raise they want, not the performance they saw. Decouple scoring from compensation and you get honest assessments.

## Context (private, not for publication)
In NALA's previous performance cycle (Spring 2026), some execs wanted to change scores after seeing the compensation implications. Owen's EYS framework deliberately separates the two: managers score purely on performance (Deliverables, Skills, Values), the CFO sets a reward budget, and the mapping is mechanical. Inspired by the QuantumLight/Revolut playbook where this principle operates at 6,000+ employees.

## The insight
Most performance systems are incentive-incompatible. The person assessing performance can see the financial outcome of their assessment at scoring time. This creates a perverse loop: the manager thinks "Sarah deserves a raise" and works backward to the score that delivers it, rather than scoring Sarah honestly and letting the compensation system handle the rest.

The fix is structural, not cultural. You can't train managers to ignore financial outcomes they can see. Instead, separate the two processes completely:

1. Managers score on performance dimensions (what did they deliver, what skills did they demonstrate, did they live the values). No financial context visible.
2. Finance sets a reward budget based on business performance. No individual scores visible.
3. A calibration layer maps scores to rewards mechanically.

The result: managers optimise for accurate assessment because that's the only lever they have. Finance optimises for sustainable spend. Neither can game the other's domain.

This works at 80 people and at 6,000. The principle is the same: make honesty the path of least resistance by removing the incentive to be strategic.

- The QuantumLight/Revolut system operates this decoupling at 6,000+ employees with documented success
- When scoring and pay are coupled, calibration sessions become negotiation sessions -- managers lobby for their people rather than assessing them

## First Draft

### Why Your Performance Reviews Are Actually Salary Negotiations in Disguise

Last review cycle, I watched a senior leader try to change a performance score after seeing what it would mean for compensation. They didn't disagree with the assessment. They agreed the person was "meeting expectations." They just didn't like the raise that implied.

So they asked if we could bump the score to "exceeding." Not because anything about the person's performance had changed. Because the financial outcome wasn't what they wanted.

This is not a story about one bad actor. This is the predictable result of a system where the person scoring performance can see the pay outcome at scoring time.

**If managers can see what their score translates to in pounds or dollars, they're not assessing performance. They're reverse-engineering a compensation outcome. Your performance review is a salary negotiation wearing an assessment costume.**

#### The incentive problem hiding in plain sight

Most performance management systems work like this: a manager rates their direct report on a scale (1-5, or "below/meets/exceeds"), and that rating maps directly to a compensation outcome. A "3" means 3% raise. A "4" means 6%. A "5" means promotion eligible.

The manager knows this mapping. Sometimes explicitly (it's in the policy document), sometimes implicitly (they've been through enough cycles to know what each rating delivers). Either way, the financial outcome is visible at scoring time.

This creates a perverse incentive loop. The manager's internal monologue isn't "what score accurately reflects this person's performance?" It's "what outcome do I want for this person, and what score gets me there?"

The manager who thinks their report deserves a bigger raise will inflate the score to deliver it. The manager under budget pressure will deflate scores to keep compensation costs down. Neither is assessing performance. Both are solving a compensation problem using the assessment as a tool.

This isn't a training problem. You can't coach managers to ignore financial information that's sitting in front of them. That's not how incentives work. If the reward for inflating a score is getting the outcome you want for your person, and the penalty is... nothing... then inflation is the rational choice.

#### What calibration looks like when scoring and pay are coupled

If you've ever been in a calibration session, you've probably seen this dynamic in action. Calibration is supposed to be the moment where managers compare assessments across the organisation and ensure consistency. In practice, when scoring drives pay, calibration becomes a negotiation table.

Managers arrive having already decided what their people "deserve" financially. The calibration session becomes a series of arguments about why each manager's people are special. The manager with the strongest advocacy skills wins the biggest raises for their team, regardless of whether those raises reflect the highest performance.

I've sat in rooms where a manager gave an impassioned five-minute speech about why their report was "exceeding expectations," and when pressed for specific evidence, couldn't name a single deliverable beyond "they're really reliable." Reliable is meeting expectations. But the manager had already committed to a compensation outcome, and they were working backward to justify it.

The person who lost out? The quiet high performer whose manager wasn't a good negotiator. Performance review systems that couple scoring to pay systematically reward the best-advocated, not the best-performing.

#### The structural fix: decouple the score from the reward

The fix is architectural, not cultural. Separate the two processes so completely that neither can contaminate the other.

**Step 1: Managers score purely on performance.**
Three dimensions, each scored independently: what did they deliver (speed, quality, complexity), what skills did they demonstrate (role-specific competencies), and did they live the values. No financial context visible. The manager's only job is accuracy.

**Step 2: Finance sets a reward budget.**
The CFO (or whoever owns compensation) sets a total reward pool based on business performance, market benchmarks, and retention priorities. This happens independently of individual scores. Finance sees the budget envelope, not the individual assessments.

**Step 3: A calibration layer maps scores to rewards mechanically.**
Once scores are calibrated and the budget is set, a mechanical mapping connects them. The top tier gets disproportionately more. The middle tier gets a standard adjustment. The bottom tier gets nothing or a development plan. No manager has a hand on both levers.

The result: managers optimise for the only thing they control -- honest assessment. Finance optimises for sustainable spend. The calibration session becomes about accuracy ("is this really a top-tier performer, and here's the evidence"), not advocacy ("my person deserves more").

#### Why the gap between tiers needs to be enormous

One thing this structure makes possible that coupled systems can't: exponential reward curves.

When scoring drives pay linearly (3% for a 3, 6% for a 4), the difference between meeting expectations and exceeding them is marginal. A top performer gets twice the raise of an average performer. That's not enough to drive the behaviour change you want.

When you decouple the systems, you can set the reward curve to whatever shape the business needs. In frameworks I've studied that operate at 6,000+ employees, the multipliers look like this:

| Grade | Bonus multiplier |
|-------|-----------------|
| Below expectations | 0.0x |
| Meets expectations | 0.5x |
| Exceeds expectations | 3.0x |
| Exceptional | 5.0x |

The gap between 0.5x and 3.0x is the whole point. It makes the difference between average and excellent feel enormous. It gives top performers a reason to stay and keep performing. And it gives average performers a visible incentive to improve.

This curve is impossible to implement when managers control both the score and the financial outcome, because they'll game the scores to avoid the discomfort of giving someone a 0.5x multiplier. Decoupling makes the hard calls structurally inevitable rather than personally uncomfortable.

#### This works at 80 people

The objection I hear most: "That's a big-company process. We're 80 people. We don't need that kind of structure."

You don't need the full apparatus. You need the principle. And the principle is simple: the person assessing performance should not be able to see the financial outcome at scoring time.

At a small company, that means the Head of People or the CFO owns the score-to-compensation mapping, and managers don't see it until after calibration is complete. That's it. One structural change. No new tools, no new process, no training programme. Just a firewall between the assessment and the reward.

The companies that get this wrong aren't the ones without process. They're the ones where process exists but the scoring and compensation levers sit in the same hands. Fix that, and you fix the single biggest source of inflation, frustration, and unfairness in your review cycle.

**What to do about it:**
- **Audit your current system.** Can managers see the compensation outcome of their scores at scoring time? If yes, your reviews are salary negotiations.
- **Separate the score from the pay.** Managers score on performance dimensions only. Finance sets the reward budget. A calibration layer connects them.
- **Make the mapping mechanical.** No manager should have discretion over both the assessment and the financial outcome for the same person.
- **Set an exponential reward curve.** The gap between "meets" and "exceeds" should feel enormous. If it doesn't, top performers have no reason to clear the higher bar.
