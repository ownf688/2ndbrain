# TPO Style Guide: What World-Class Looks Like

Research-backed writing guide for The People Operator, synthesized from structural analysis of 40+ professional blogs/newsletters including Lenny Rachitsky, Jessica Zwaan, Hung Lee, Adam Grant, Julie Zhuo, Wes Kao, DHH, Josh Bersin, Claire Hughes Johnson, First Round Review, Lars Schmidt, Kim Scott, and performance data from Edelman-LinkedIn, Semrush, and SEO.co studies.

## The TPO positioning

Owen writes as a **practitioner-operator**, not an analyst, consultant, or academic. The voice is someone who just did the thing, has the data to prove it, and has an opinion about what it means. This maps to the highest-performing archetype in B2B thought leadership:

| Archetype | Example | Works because |
|-----------|---------|---------------|
| Academic | Josh Bersin, Adam Grant | Research authority, translates studies |
| Consultant | McKinsey blog, SHRM | Breadth, safety, institutional voice |
| Curator | Hung Lee, Lars Schmidt | Saves time, trusted filter |
| **Operator** | **Lenny, Zwaan, Wes Kao, DHH** | **"I did this. Here's what happened."** |

Operators win because they have something the other archetypes can't fake: **first-party evidence**. Owen has data from running a People function at a scaling fintech (50-200 people), building AI-native tooling, and making real decisions with real consequences. That's the moat.

## Length guidelines

| Format | Word count | When to use |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| **LinkedIn post** | 200-350 words | Single insight, one take, no framework needed |
| **Opinion piece** | 1,200-1,800 words | One claim, strong evidence, actionable close |
| **Deep dive** | 2,500-3,500 words | Framework + data + war story, flagship content |
| **Research piece** | 4,000-5,000 words | Original data, taxonomy, reference-grade |

Data backing: B2B thought leadership sweet spot is 1,000-1,500 words for opinion (Semrush 2026), 2,000-3,000 for comprehensive (SEO.co), 3,000-4,000 for reference (First Round Review). Posts under 600 words get more comments but fewer shares. Posts over 3,000 get 3x more shares than sub-1,000. For TPO, default to **1,500-2,500 words** -- deep enough to be credible, tight enough to finish in one sitting.

## The structure that works

Synthesized from the best-performing patterns across the research sample:

### 1. The Hook (2-3 sentences)

The first 210 characters determine whether anyone reads the rest. Five hook types that work:

| Hook type | Example | When to use |
|-----------|---------|-------------|
| **Data bomb** | "We tracked 16 managers for 30 days. 12 had zero feedback despite weekly 1:1s." | You have a surprising number |
| **Pain mirror** | "You know that manager who 'doesn't have time' for feedback? They had 112 one-on-ones last month." | Reader will recognise the problem |
| **Confessional** | "I launched a feedback initiative and then discovered my own team wasn't giving feedback." | Vulnerability + credibility |
| **Contrarian claim** | "1:1 frequency is a vanity metric. Stop tracking it." | You're challenging conventional wisdom |
| **Story mid-action** | "The email landed at 9am: three engineers had just resigned." | Narrative tension |

What never works: dictionary definitions, broad industry trends, "In this article I will...", hedged openings.

### 2. The Setup (1 paragraph, 50-80 words)

Why this matters. What's at stake. Who should care. Frame the problem before offering the solution. Adam Grant's formula: connect to behaviour people already recognise (work habits, leadership mistakes, hiring patterns).

### 3. The Thesis (1 bold sentence)

The screenshot-worthy line. This is the "take" from the TPO seed. It should be a claim, not a question. Opinionated, specific, falsifiable. A reader should be able to disagree with it.

Good: "If managers can see the pay outcome while scoring, they're not assessing performance -- they're lobbying for compensation."
Bad: "Performance management is really important and companies should think about it more."

### 4. The Evidence (2-4 sections, 800-1,500 words total)

This is where operator credibility lives. Each section follows: **Claim > Evidence > Implication**.

The evidence hierarchy (strongest to weakest):
1. **Original data from your work** -- "We tracked 16 managers..." (unbeatable)
2. **Named framework you built** -- "The values-veto principle" (shareable, memorable)
3. **Specific war story, anonymised** -- "A fintech scaling from 50 to 200..." (relatable)
4. **External research that validates** -- "The Edelman study found 86% of buyers..." (borrowed authority)
5. **Analogy or metaphor** -- "The meeting is the alibi" (sticky but needs backing)

Ratio that works: 60% original evidence, 30% frameworks/models, 10% external validation. Lead with your stuff. The reader came for your experience, not a literature review.

First Round Review's non-negotiable: **every piece must contain at least one thing the reader can implement today**. Not "think about" -- implement.

### 5. The Framework (optional, but high-leverage)

If there's a repeatable model, **name it**. Named frameworks get shared. Kim Scott's "Radical Candor" started as a single diagram in a First Round Review article. Lenny's "Racecar Growth Framework" became industry vocabulary.

Rules for frameworks:
- Simple enough to draw on a whiteboard
- Has 2-4 components (not 7, not 12)
- Solves a problem the reader has this week
- Name is descriptive, not clever ("The Values Veto" not "The VVX Model")

### 6. The "So What" (1 paragraph)

Why this changes how the reader should think or act. This is where the universal insight lands. Strip all company-specific detail. Write for the Head of People at any 50-500 person company.

### 7. The Action (2-4 bullets)

What to do Monday morning. Specific. Tactical. Not "think about your feedback culture" but "pull your manager feedback data for the last 30 days and count how many gave zero."

Wes Kao's principle: close with stakes reinforcement, not summary. "Don't waste this lever" beats "In conclusion, feedback is important."

## Voice and tone

Synthesised from what works across the top operators:

**Do:**
- Write in first person. "I" not "one" or "we" (unless it was genuinely a team effort)
- Lead with the answer, then the reasoning
- Use short declarative sentences alongside longer analytical ones (rhythm)
- Be specific: names (anonymised), numbers, dates, tools
- Admit what you got wrong before showing what you got right
- State your confidence level when the evidence is thin

**Don't:**
- Hedge with "I think" or "in my opinion" -- the byline already says it's your opinion
- Use HR jargon (human capital, talent acquisition, employer branding) -- write in plain English
- Open with "In today's fast-paced world..." or any variation of it
- Use emdashes (Owen rule: they signal AI-generated text)
- Write for other People leaders. Write for operators who happen to run People functions

**Owen's specific voice markers:**
- Direct. Punchy. No corporate fluff.
- Sentences are short. Active voice.
- Leads with the ask or the decision, not the background.
- Contractions always. "Don't" not "do not".
- Numbers, not adjectives. "12 of 16 managers" not "the vast majority of managers".

## Paragraph rhythm (from Lenny analysis)

Paragraph length isn't random. The best pieces alternate deliberately:

- **Short (1-2 sentences):** Emotional beats, conclusions, transitions. "It was pretty awful." / "That was zero."
- **Medium (3-5 sentences):** The workhorse. Explanation, context, analysis.
- **Long (6-10 sentences):** Reserved for deep technical detail or complex war stories. Never two in a row.

**The pattern:** Short > Medium > Medium > Short > Long > Short. This creates rest stops that prevent fatigue. Short paragraphs after long ones feel like relief, not thinness.

## The reusable artifact rule (from Lenny + First Round Review)

Every piece should produce one artifact that can be shared independently of the prose:
- A named framework ("The Values Veto", "The Proxy Trap")
- A table or comparison matrix
- A checklist ("4 things to audit Monday")
- A diagnostic question ("Can your managers see the pay outcome at scoring time?")

Named frameworks are the #1 driver of virality in the People/HR space (from comparative analysis of 18 writers). "Give Away Your Legos" (Molly Graham), "Radical Candor" (Kim Scott), "Superworker" (Bersin) all started as single articles with a named concept. **If you can't name it, it won't travel.**

## The failure-first arc

Lenny's most popular piece of all time opens with failure. The Duolingo growth story starts with "user growth was slowing down," not "here's how we 4.5x'd DAU." Jessica Zwaan opens with "I hate engagement surveys" before proposing an alternative.

**Pattern:** Problem/failure > What I tried > What actually worked > The framework > What to do.

This is counterintuitive for thought leadership (most writers lead with wins) but it's the strongest trust-builder. Opening with failure signals "I was in the arena." It earns the right to teach.

For TPO: always open from a position of honest experience, not polished authority. "I tracked 16 managers and discovered my own team wasn't giving feedback" is stronger than "Here's how to build a feedback culture."

## Headlines and titles

Data: numbers in titles increase CTR by 36%. Brackets increase clicks by 38%. Clear beats clever by 5x.

**Formula:** [Claim] or [Number] + [Specific promise] + [Bracket qualifier]

Good:
- "Your Managers Are Meeting, Not Coaching -- Here's How to Tell"
- "We Tracked 16 Managers for 30 Days. Only 4 Gave Feedback."
- "Values Are a Veto, Not a Dimension [How to Make Them Bite]"

Bad:
- "Thoughts on Feedback Culture in Modern Organisations"
- "The Future of Performance Management"
- "Why HR Needs to Change"

The title should be the thesis, compressed. If you can't turn the title into a take, the piece doesn't have a clear enough thesis.

## Publishing cadence

Data: weekly is the floor for serious newsletter growth (Lenny, Wes Kao, Hung Lee all weekly). Consistency matters more than frequency. Missing your cadence is more damaging than posting less often.

**Recommended for TPO launch:** biweekly (every 2 weeks). One piece every 2 weeks is sustainable alongside a full-time Head of People role. Build the backlog to 4-6 pieces before publishing the first one, so the cadence is locked in from day one.

## What separates world-class from good

| Dimension | Good | World-class |
|-----------|------|-------------|
| **Data** | Cites others' research | Generates original data from own work |
| **Voice** | Professional, correct | Distinctive -- could only come from this person |
| **Evidence** | "Many companies struggle with..." | "We tracked 16 managers. 12 had zero feedback." |
| **Framework** | Describes a concept | Names it, makes it visual, makes it shareable |
| **Vulnerability** | Shares wins | Opens with what went wrong first |
| **Action** | "Think about your approach" | "Pull this data. Run this query. Ask this question." |
| **Thesis** | Covers a topic | Makes a claim someone could disagree with |
| **Length** | Writes to fill space | Every sentence earns its place |
