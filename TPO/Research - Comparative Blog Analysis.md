# Structural Analysis: How the Best People/HR/Leadership Writers Write

*Compiled July 2026. Analysis of 18 writers/publications across the People, HR, talent, leadership, and org design space. Focus is on HOW they write, not what.*

---

## Part 1: Individual Writer Profiles

### 1. Wes Kao (Newsletter on managing up / executive communication)
- **Word count**: ~1,500-2,500 words per issue. Bi-weekly on Wednesdays.
- **Opening move**: Drops the reader straight into a workplace scenario or bold claim. Uses BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front) -- the punchline comes first, context follows. Her own framework: "start right before you get eaten by the bear" -- no throat-clearing.
- **Structure**: Numbered principles or titled sub-sections. Heavy use of **bold text** as signposts so readers can skim. Before/after examples are a signature device (bad email vs. good email, weak framing vs. strong framing). Bullet lists with bold lead-ins.
- **Close**: Usually a tight restatement of the core principle, often a single-sentence kicker.
- **Mix**: 70% framework, 25% workplace anecdote, 5% data. Almost zero academic citation.
- **Tone**: Direct, surgical, zero-BS. Practitioner speaking to practitioners. Not warm, not cold -- precise. Like a coach giving notes between reps.
- **Shareability driver**: The before/after transformation. Readers screenshot the "bad vs. good" examples and share them on LinkedIn because they see themselves in the "before."
- **Signature moves**: Bold text as scannable signposts. Concrete before/after rewrites. Numbered lists of principles (5, 7, 15). Short paragraphs (1-3 sentences max). Tactical specificity -- never vague advice.

### 2. Adam Grant (Granted newsletter / NYT opinion / organizational psychology)
- **Word count**: Newsletter ~1,000-1,500. NYT opinion pieces ~800-1,200. Monthly newsletter cadence.
- **Opening move**: Leads with a surprising research finding or a counterintuitive claim that challenges conventional wisdom. "You think X is true. The data says otherwise." Sometimes opens with a vivid human story that becomes the vehicle for the research.
- **Structure**: Short essay format. No headers or numbered lists in the newsletter -- flowing prose. NYT pieces follow classic opinion structure: hook, thesis, evidence blocks, turn, close.
- **Close**: Lands on a reframe -- the reader should see the world slightly differently. Often ends with a memorable one-liner or a call to intellectual humility.
- **Mix**: 45% research/data, 35% storytelling, 20% framework. Always cites studies. The research IS the content.
- **Tone**: Warm academic. Accessible but credentialed. "I'm a professor but I'm going to make this fun." Conversational without being casual. "Arguing like I'm right, listening like I'm wrong."
- **Shareability driver**: The "did you know?" factor. People share Grant because his pieces make them feel smarter. The counterintuitive finding is the share trigger.
- **Signature moves**: Research-first persuasion. Study + story + reframe in every piece. Humor woven lightly. Reader Q&A in newsletter. Groups ideas into threes.

### 3. Josh Bersin (HR industry analyst blog)
- **Word count**: Ranges wildly -- 700-word market commentary to 1,200-word thought pieces. Publishes 2-4x per week.
- **Opening move**: Drops the reader into market context or a news hook. "The explosive AI market is changing everything..." Direct, declarative, assumes the reader is a senior HR buyer or executive.
- **Structure**: Flowing narrative with bold subheadings every 150-250 words. Uses embedded charts/diagrams. Often includes "Additional Information" sections linking to deeper research. No numbered lists -- prefers analytical prose.
- **Close**: Forward-looking prediction + commercial call-to-action (certifications, reports, tools). Always converts insight into a next step.
- **Mix**: 35% data/research, 45% framework/market analysis, 20% anecdote (company examples). Cites his own proprietary research heavily.
- **Tone**: Authoritative analyst. Professional skepticism mixed with pragmatic optimism. Slightly paternalistic ("I don't want to rain on any parades"). Industry elder statesman energy.
- **Shareability driver**: Market intelligence. HR leaders share Bersin to signal they're current. The proprietary data and named company examples give readers ammunition for internal conversations.
- **Signature moves**: Historical parallels (database market to AI market). Proprietary frameworks with branded names ("Superworker," "Systemic HR"). Embedded research graphics. Cross-promotion to podcasts and reports. Title Case For Every Heading Word.

### 4. Julie Zhuo (The Looking Glass -- management/leadership essays)
- **Word count**: 1,500-3,000 words. Monthly-ish cadence ("once-a-month-ish musings").
- **Opening move**: Personal story or vivid interior moment. "I felt suddenly awake, like Dorothy seeing color for the first time." Starts with feeling, not fact. The opening is almost always a first-person emotional experience.
- **Structure**: Long-form essay with no headers or minimal headers. Three-part structure: product/design, leadership/team, personal growth -- often woven into a single theme. No bullet points. Prose-forward.
- **Close**: Reflective, often circling back to the opening image or feeling. Ends on a note of earned wisdom rather than action items.
- **Mix**: 60% personal anecdote/reflection, 25% framework, 15% observation. Almost zero data citation.
- **Tone**: Introspective, literary, vulnerable. Reads more like personal essay than business writing. Warm and generous. Started zanier (parenthetical asides, absurd scenarios) and evolved toward more conventional clarity.
- **Shareability driver**: Emotional recognition. Readers share Zhuo because she articulates something they felt but couldn't name. The vulnerability is the draw.
- **Signature moves**: Personal confession as teaching vehicle. Extended metaphors. Literary quality prose. Italics for internal voice. Monthly reflection format. "Once-a-month-ish" cadence signals no obligation.

### 5. Lars Schmidt (Redefining Work newsletter)
- **Word count**: ~1,000-1,500. Weekly on Wednesdays, though shifting toward "publish when I have something worth saying."
- **Opening move**: Practitioner context-setting. Writes from inside the work -- "what I'm wrestling with right now." Signals with practical credibility rather than research or hot takes.
- **Structure**: Essay format with light subheadings. Curated links and analysis. Mix of original analysis and commentary on industry developments.
- **Close**: Forward-looking or community-oriented. Points to conversations happening in his ecosystem (podcast guests, cohort learnings).
- **Mix**: 40% practitioner insight, 35% industry analysis, 25% curated commentary. Light on academic data, heavy on pattern recognition from consulting.
- **Tone**: Practitioner-insider. Anti-corporate-jargon. Anti-"thought leadership." Writes about what he's actually doing, not theorizing. Grounded, direct, no performance.
- **Shareability driver**: Insider credibility. HR leaders share Schmidt because he's a known practitioner, not a pundit. Sharing him signals you're in the "modern HR" tribe.
- **Signature moves**: Explicit rejection of hype. "Practical implications, not breathless coverage." Podcast episode tie-ins. Community-building through cohort references.

### 6. Claire Hughes Johnson (Scaling People / Stripe COO articles)
- **Word count**: Long-form: 3,000-5,000+ (First Round Review style). Book excerpts and templates.
- **Opening move**: Problem statement grounded in scaling experience. "When we were at X employees, we realized Y wasn't working." Credentials front-loaded through company name-drops (Google, Stripe).
- **Structure**: Highly structured with clear headers. Template/worksheet format within articles. Alternates between narrative and actionable artifacts (checklists, exercises, example documents). 100+ pages of worksheets in the book.
- **Close**: Practical summary with a downloadable or actionable artifact. "Here's the template. Use it Monday."
- **Mix**: 50% tactical framework/templates, 30% scaling anecdotes, 20% principles. Minimal external data -- the authority comes from the companies she built.
- **Tone**: Executive operator. Professional, precise, generous with specifics. Not academic, not warm-fuzzy -- operational. "Here's how we actually did it at Stripe."
- **Shareability driver**: The templates. People share because they can literally copy-paste the artifact into their own company. Operational gold.
- **Signature moves**: Templates embedded in narrative. "Working With Claire" documents. Operating principles as content. Worksheets at end of each section.

### 7. Molly Graham (Lessons newsletter -- scaling companies)
- **Word count**: ~1,500-2,500. Regular Substack cadence.
- **Opening move**: Personal story or emotional confession. Opens with vulnerability -- miscarriages, fear, chaos. Then pivots to the metaphor that becomes the article's spine.
- **Structure**: Narrative essay built around a central metaphor. Light on headers. The metaphor IS the structure. Sometimes phases/stages (employee count ranges).
- **Close**: Returns to the personal. Often ends with what she's still learning, not what she's figured out.
- **Mix**: 50% personal anecdote, 35% framework/metaphor, 15% company examples. Zero data.
- **Tone**: Deeply personal, generous, emotionally honest. "Lover of weird metaphors." Never performative. Reads like a letter from a friend who's been through it.
- **Shareability driver**: The metaphors. "Give Away Your Legos" became a vocabulary item. People share Molly because her metaphors give them language for feelings they couldn't articulate.
- **Signature moves**: Signature metaphors as brand (Legos, cliff jumping, Waterline Model, Sandwich Metaphor). Personal vulnerability as authority. "I'm still figuring this out" energy. Phases organized by employee count.

### 8. DHH / David Heinemeier Hansson (world.hey.com -- contrarian management)
- **Word count**: 600-1,200 words. Publishes multiple times per week.
- **Opening move**: Provocative declarative statement. "Europe is finally waking up." No preamble, no throat-clearing, no "in today's fast-paced world." Drops you into the argument mid-stride.
- **Structure**: Single thesis, no headers, no bullet points. Pure argumentative prose. One idea, driven home relentlessly. Short paragraphs. Reads like a well-formed rant.
- **Close**: Lands on a sharp conclusion that reframes the opening claim. Often ends with an implicit "and everyone else is wrong about this."
- **Mix**: 20% data/evidence, 30% contrarian argument, 50% opinion/conviction. Cites nothing -- authority comes from building Basecamp for 20+ years.
- **Tone**: Contrarian provocateur. Confident to the point of combative. Anti-hustle, anti-hype, anti-consensus. "I've been right about this for a decade."
- **Shareability driver**: The controversy. People share DHH to either signal agreement with his contrarian stance or to argue with it. Both work.
- **Signature moves**: Short, punchy posts. Zero formatting (no bold, no lists, no headers). Pure prose. Deliberate resistance to trends (anti-AI hype, anti-meeting culture, pro-40-hour-week). Uses his company as the living proof.

### 9. Kim Scott / Radical Candor (blog)
- **Word count**: 1,500-2,800 words.
- **Opening move**: Personal story from Apple or Google. Establishes credibility through named-executive anecdotes (Sheryl Sandberg features prominently). The story reveals the framework.
- **Structure**: Story first, then framework introduction (the 2x2 quadrant), then tactical application. Uses the quadrant as visual anchor. Sections organized by the four quadrants or by actionable tactics.
- **Close**: Vulnerability or self-care note. Often connects professional framework to personal wellness.
- **Mix**: 60% personal anecdote, 30% framework, 10% data. The stories ARE the teaching.
- **Tone**: Candid (on brand), warm, self-deprecating. Funny. Conversational without being sloppy. Admits mistakes openly.
- **Shareability driver**: The 2x2 framework itself. People share the quadrant diagram constantly. The framework is so simple it becomes common vocabulary ("that's Ruinous Empathy").
- **Signature moves**: Named 2x2 quadrant (Radical Candor / Ruinous Empathy / Obnoxious Aggression / Manipulative Insincerity). Acronyms (HHIPP). Personal stories from famous companies. Visual diagrams. Workplace comedy series (The Feedback Loop).

### 10. Erin Meyer (culture/management -- HBR articles)
- **Word count**: 2,000-4,000 (HBR format).
- **Opening move**: Cross-cultural anecdote that creates cognitive dissonance. A manager does something that works perfectly in one culture and fails spectacularly in another. The gap between expectations and reality IS the hook.
- **Structure**: Classic HBR format -- problem, framework, evidence, application. Uses scales/spectrums (the Culture Map's 8 dimensions). Case studies organized by cultural comparison.
- **Close**: Practical guidance organized by leadership behavior (communicating, evaluating, persuading, etc.).
- **Mix**: 40% cross-cultural anecdotes, 35% framework (the 8 scales), 25% research. Blends psychology and anthropology.
- **Tone**: Academic-practitioner hybrid. INSEAD professor energy. Authoritative but curious. Treats cultural differences as fascinating puzzles, not problems.
- **Shareability driver**: The "aha" of cultural comparison. People share Meyer because they suddenly understand why that meeting in Tokyo went sideways. Pattern recognition across cultures.
- **Signature moves**: 8-dimension Culture Map as recurring visual. Country comparisons as narrative spine. "Dilemma-based" culture definition. Spectrum/scale thinking rather than binary categories.

### 11. Pat Lencioni / The Table Group (leadership blog + books)
- **Word count**: Blog posts ~500-800. Books: fable format.
- **Opening move**: Scenario or fable-style setup. Often starts with a relatable leadership frustration, then introduces the concept that resolves it.
- **Structure**: Fable + framework. Books tell a story, then extract the model. Blog posts ("Pat's Point of View") are shorter, opinion-forward riffs on organizational health themes.
- **Close**: Calls the reader to self-examine. "Which of these dysfunctions is your team stuck on?"
- **Mix**: 50% fable/narrative, 40% framework, 10% practical application. No academic data.
- **Tone**: Accessible, earnest, slightly pastoral. Speaks to mid-level leaders with genuine care. Not trendy, not provocative. Timeless rather than timely.
- **Shareability driver**: The frameworks stick. "Five Dysfunctions of a Team" is corporate vocabulary. People share Lencioni because the models are diagnostic -- they explain what's broken.
- **Signature moves**: Business fable format (story first, framework extracted). Named models with numbered components (Five Dysfunctions, Six Types of Working Genius). Self-assessment built into the content. Podcast conversations as extension.

### 12. First Round Review (startup people/management)
- **Word count**: 4,000-6,000 words. Long-form, interview-based.
- **Opening move**: Bio-driven credibility setup. "Jane Doe scaled Company X from 25 to 5,000 employees." Then a narrative hook that makes you care about the person's specific insight.
- **Structure**: Interview transformed into narrative. Clean headers every 400-600 words. Pull quotes as visual breaks. "Founder's Notepad" sidebars with external quotes. Alternating narrative + template/checklist sections.
- **Close**: Practical checklist or downloadable artifact. Sometimes a curated collection of pull quotes from other leaders.
- **Mix**: 50% framework/tactical advice, 35% anecdote, 15% data. Everything is sourced from interviews -- "personality-driven" content.
- **Tone**: Professional but accessible. Reads like a well-edited magazine feature. Authoritative without being stuffy. "Here's exactly how to do this" energy.
- **Shareability driver**: The tactical depth. People share First Round because the articles are reference documents. You bookmark them and come back.
- **Signature moves**: Pull quotes in offset formatting. 5,000+ word depth. Interview-to-narrative transformation. Bolded key phrases. Checklists and artifacts embedded. Related content ecosystem.

### 13. Lenny Rachitsky (Lenny's Newsletter -- product/growth/people)
- **Word count**: Variable but substantial. 10-30 hours per post. Twice weekly.
- **Opening move**: Hook (1-2 sentences) with blunt insight or surprising result. Then context (2-3 sentences). Then tactical breakdown (3-5 bullets or short paragraphs). BLUF structure similar to Wes Kao but warmer.
- **Structure**: Hook > Context > Tactical Breakdown > Deeper sections with headers. Uses tables, embedded frameworks, guest contributions. Mixes essay with advice-column format (reader Q&A).
- **Close**: Summary of key takeaways. Often a "teach the reader" section (50-75 words) that translates wins into something transferable.
- **Mix**: 60% tactical framework, 25% expert interview/quote, 15% data. Density of high-quality advice over volume of prose.
- **Tone**: Helpful friend who happens to know everyone. Warm, generous, zero ego. "I did the research so you don't have to." Anti-pontification.
- **Shareability driver**: The utility. People share Lenny because his posts are immediately usable. The embedded frameworks and templates become team resources.
- **Signature moves**: Guest expert frameworks embedded in essays. Tables and visual summaries. Reader Q&A format. "Best of" annual roundups. Podcast-to-newsletter pipeline.

### 14. Dan Rockwell / Leadership Freak (daily leadership blog)
- **Word count**: 300 words or less. Daily.
- **Opening move**: Single leadership observation or quote. No setup, no story. Drops you into the insight immediately.
- **Structure**: Micro-format. One idea per post. Sometimes a short numbered list (5-7 items). No headers needed at this length.
- **Close**: One-liner or question for reflection.
- **Mix**: 80% distilled wisdom/principles, 15% observation, 5% data. Pure insight compression.
- **Tone**: Motivational elder. Earnest, direct, warm. Never ironic, never contrarian. Daily devotional energy for leaders.
- **Shareability driver**: The brevity. 300 words is screenshot-friendly. People share Rockwell because it takes 60 seconds to read and the insight is immediately quotable.
- **Signature moves**: 300-word ceiling. Daily cadence (365 days/year). Quote-driven. Listicles compressed to micro-format. "Empowering leaders 300 words at a time" as brand promise.

### 15. HR Brew (newsletter -- Morning Brew for HR)
- **Word count**: ~500-800 per edition (multiple short items). Daily.
- **Opening move**: Witty editor note. Then headline + image + body text for each story. News-first, personality-second.
- **Structure**: Headline > image > body text, repeat. Multiple stories per edition. Skimmable by design. Each item is 100-200 words. Editor initials on each section.
- **Close**: Lighter item or culture reference. Often a gif or meme.
- **Mix**: 50% news analysis, 30% industry context, 20% editorial commentary. Minimal original research.
- **Tone**: Informal without being unprofessional. Millennial humor. Empathetic but never condescending. "Boring topics don't require boring writing."
- **Shareability driver**: The tone. People forward HR Brew because it makes HR news not painful to read. The humor is the differentiator.
- **Signature moves**: Gifs and memes embedded. Editor initials on each item. Witty headlines. Morning Brew house style (accessible, fun, skimmable). Daily cadence creates habit.

### 16. ERE / TLNT (recruiting publications)
- **Word count**: 800-1,500. Contributor-driven.
- **Opening move**: Problem statement relevant to a TA leader's daily reality. Practical and specific.
- **Structure**: Clear sections with headers. Tactical advice organized into steps or categories. Clean layout, search-friendly.
- **Close**: Actionable takeaways or checklist.
- **Mix**: 40% tactical advice, 30% industry trend analysis, 30% contributor expertise. Mix of data and practitioner voice.
- **Tone**: Professional practitioner. No frills. Content designed to be immediately applicable in a recruiting team's Monday meeting.
- **Shareability driver**: SEO-driven discoverability more than viral sharing. People find ERE/TLNT when searching for specific recruiting problems.
- **Signature moves**: Contributor expertise model. Multi-part series for complex topics. C-suite to practitioner range. Pioneer credibility (founded 1998).

### 17. NOBL Academy (org design)
- **Word count**: Variable -- short toolkit pieces (500-800) to deeper essays (1,500-2,500).
- **Opening move**: Defines or redefines a concept. "What is organizational design?" Then immediately makes it tangible.
- **Structure**: Toolkit format. Concept > framework > tool > application. The Organizational Charter (5 domains: Environment, Purpose, Strategies, Structures, Systems) is the recurring spine.
- **Close**: Often points to a downloadable tool or framework.
- **Mix**: 60% framework/toolkit, 25% case study, 15% principles. Design-thinking influence is everywhere.
- **Tone**: Consultant-educator. Clean, modern, slightly academic. Design-thinking vocabulary. More "organization as system" than "leader as hero."
- **Shareability driver**: The tools. People share NOBL because the frameworks are immediately deployable in a workshop or offsite.
- **Signature moves**: Open-source knowledge ethos. Five-domain charter as recurring model. Design thinking applied to org problems. Iterative/experimental language.

### 18. People Managing People (blog)
- **Word count**: 1,500-3,000 (SEO-optimized long-form).
- **Opening move**: Problem statement framed as a question an HR leader would actually ask. SEO-aware but not robotic.
- **Structure**: Clear headings (H2/H3). 2-4 main sections. Real-life examples and case studies. Charts and data visualizations encouraged. Summary or actionable checklist at close.
- **Close**: Specific takeaways or short actionable checklist.
- **Mix**: 40% tactical guidance, 30% data/research citation, 30% practitioner insight. More data-forward than most.
- **Tone**: Peer-to-peer professional. "Speak to readers as equals -- professional, direct, personable." Helps rather than lectures.
- **Shareability driver**: Comprehensive coverage. People share PMP articles as reference guides when a colleague asks "how do I handle X?"
- **Signature moves**: Short paragraphs (5 lines max, not 5 sentences). Recent credible data with direct links. Visual data. Newsletter tie-in. Resource hub structure.

## Part 2: Cross-Cutting Patterns

### Pattern 1: The Four Archetypes

Every writer in this space falls into one of four structural archetypes:

| Archetype | Writers | Core Move |
|-----------|---------|-----------|
| **The Framework Builder** | Wes Kao, Kim Scott, Erin Meyer, NOBL, Lencioni | Creates a named model (2x2, numbered list, scale) that becomes shared vocabulary |
| **The Story-First Essayist** | Julie Zhuo, Molly Graham, Lencioni (books) | Personal narrative that surfaces a universal truth through specific experience |
| **The Analyst-Insider** | Josh Bersin, Lars Schmidt, ERE/TLNT, HR Brew | Market intelligence + practitioner pattern recognition = insider credibility |
| **The Research Translator** | Adam Grant, First Round Review, Claire Hughes Johnson | Takes evidence (academic or experiential) and makes it actionable for operators |

The best writers blend two archetypes. Molly Graham is Story-First + Framework Builder. Bersin is Analyst + Framework Builder. First Round Review is Research Translator + Framework Builder.

### Pattern 2: The Opening Move Spectrum

Writers open on a spectrum from **emotional** to **analytical**:

```
EMOTIONAL <<<------------------------------------------>>> ANALYTICAL

Julie Zhuo   Molly Graham   Kim Scott   Wes Kao   Lars Schmidt   Josh Bersin   DHH
(personal    (vulnerable     (famous-    (bold     (practitioner  (market       (provocative
 moment)      confession)     company     claim)    context)       data)          thesis)
                              story)
```

Every successful opener does ONE thing: creates a gap the reader needs closed. The gap is either:
- **Emotional**: "I've felt this too" (Zhuo, Graham)
- **Cognitive**: "You thought X, but actually Y" (Grant, DHH)
- **Tactical**: "Here's the thing you're doing wrong" (Wes Kao)
- **Status**: "Here's what the best companies know" (Bersin, First Round)

### Pattern 3: Word Count Clusters

Three distinct word count bands dominate:

| Band | Range | Writers | Use Case |
|------|-------|---------|----------|
| **Micro** | 300-800 | Dan Rockwell, HR Brew, DHH, Lencioni blog | Daily consumption, screenshot-friendly, quote-worthy |
| **Standard** | 1,000-2,500 | Wes Kao, Adam Grant, Lars Schmidt, Josh Bersin, Molly Graham, Kim Scott | Core newsletter/blog format. Deep enough to be useful, short enough to finish |
| **Long-form** | 3,000-6,000 | First Round Review, Claire Hughes Johnson, Erin Meyer (HBR) | Reference documents. Bookmarked, not just read. Template/artifact-rich |

The sweet spot for shareability is **1,200-2,000 words** with strong visual breaks. But the most bookmarked/referenced pieces tend to be 3,000+ with embedded artifacts.

### Pattern 4: What Makes Pieces Shareable (Ranked)

1. **A named framework people can use as vocabulary** -- "Give Away Your Legos," "Radical Candor," "Superworker." If you name it, people spread it.
2. **Before/after transformation** -- Wes Kao's bad-email-vs-good-email screenshots get shared more than her best prose.
3. **The counterintuitive finding** -- Adam Grant's "you think X, data says Y" gets shared because it makes the sharer look smart.
4. **A downloadable/copy-paste artifact** -- Claire Hughes Johnson's templates, First Round's checklists. Utility drives forwarding.
5. **Emotional recognition** -- Julie Zhuo and Molly Graham get shared with "this is exactly how I feel" energy.
6. **Controversy/provocation** -- DHH gets shared by both allies and opponents. Polarization drives distribution.
7. **Market intelligence** -- Bersin gets shared in Slack channels with "FYI -- worth reading" by people signaling they're current.

### Pattern 6: Closing Move Taxonomy

| Close Type | Who Does It | Effect |
|-----------|-------------|--------|
| **The Artifact** | Claire Hughes Johnson, First Round, NOBL | "Download this template" -- converts reading into action |
| **The Kicker** | Wes Kao, DHH | One sharp sentence that reframes everything -- memorable |
| **The Reflection** | Julie Zhuo, Molly Graham | Circles back to the opening feeling -- creates emotional resonance |
| **The Prediction** | Josh Bersin | "Here's where this is going" -- positions reader for the future |
| **The Self-Exam** | Lencioni, Kim Scott | "Which quadrant are you in?" -- makes it personal |
| **The Reframe** | Adam Grant | "Now think about X differently" -- intellectual shift |

### Pattern 7: Formatting as Brand

The most distinctive writers have a visual signature you could identify without the byline:

- **Wes Kao**: Bold text signposts + before/after rewrites. Unmistakable.
- **DHH**: Zero formatting. No bold, no headers, no lists. Pure prose. The absence IS the brand.
- **Josh Bersin**: Embedded charts + Title Case headers + "Additional Information" sections.
- **First Round Review**: Pull quotes in offset boxes + 5,000-word depth + Founder's Notepad.
- **Dan Rockwell**: 300 words. Period. The constraint IS the format.
- **HR Brew**: Gifs + editor initials + millennial humor. Morning Brew house style.
- **Kim Scott**: 2x2 quadrant diagrams. The visual framework IS the brand.

### Pattern 8: Frequency vs. Depth Trade-off

```
HIGH FREQUENCY                                    LOW FREQUENCY
(daily/2x week)                                   (monthly/irregular)

Dan Rockwell ---- HR Brew ---- Bersin ---- Wes Kao ---- Lars Schmidt ---- Julie Zhuo
(300 words)      (500-800)    (700-1200)  (1500-2500)  (1000-1500)       (1500-3000)
daily            daily        2-4x/week   bi-weekly     weekly-ish        monthly-ish

                    SHALLOW <-----> DEEP
```

No one succeeds at high frequency AND high depth simultaneously. The market rewards choosing a lane:
- **High frequency + shallow** = habit formation (Rockwell, HR Brew). Readers open it because it's part of their morning.
- **Low frequency + deep** = event creation (Zhuo, First Round). Each issue is an event worth clearing time for.
- **Medium frequency + medium depth** = the "standard newsletter" (Wes Kao, Lars Schmidt, Molly Graham). The reliable bi-weekly that readers look forward to.

## Part 3: Implications for Your Own Writing

### What the top performers all share (regardless of archetype):

1. **One idea per piece.** Even 5,000-word First Round articles have a single thesis. Everything serves that one idea.
2. **The opening earns the next sentence.** No throat-clearing. No "in today's fast-paced world." The first sentence must create a gap.
3. **Specificity over abstraction.** Named companies, exact employee counts, real email rewrites, actual templates. The more concrete, the more shareable.
4. **A close that does work.** Nobody just trails off. The close either reframes (Grant), provides an artifact (First Round), or lands a kicker (Wes Kao).
5. **Visual rhythm.** Short paragraphs. White space. Bold text or headers every 150-300 words. Even Julie Zhuo's flowing essays have paragraph-level rhythm.
6. **A recognizable structural signature.** Readers know what they're getting. The format itself becomes brand.

### What separates the 10K+ subscriber newsletters from the rest:

- **They named something.** Every breakout writer coined a term or framework that entered common usage.
- **They chose a lane.** Not "HR generalist" but "managing up" (Wes Kao), "scaling chaos" (Molly Graham), "HR market intelligence" (Bersin).
- **They write from inside the work**, not about it from the outside.
- **Their best piece could be understood by someone outside HR.** The pieces that cross over use universal human dynamics, not industry jargon.
