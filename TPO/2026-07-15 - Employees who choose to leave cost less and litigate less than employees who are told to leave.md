# Employees who choose to leave cost less and litigate less than employees who are told to leave

> **The take:** The choice model — two financially distinct separation paths, employee picks one — is faster, cheaper, and more legally defensible than standard redundancy. Most People leaders treat restructuring as a communication exercise. It's an offer design exercise.

## Context (private, not for publication)

NALA automating CS workload via AI. Senegal-based CS team (3 agents) being right-sized — workload has materially shrunk. Owen proposed offering a structured choice rather than issuing notice: Path A (enhanced package, mutual separation, close fast) vs Path B (statutory process, minimum entitlements). Lynnette confirmed the Senegal severance structure is viable and the packages exceed statutory minimum. Risk flagged: "without prejudice" framing not fully insulated in some African jurisdictions — needs case-law check before using that label. Josh is DRI on the FinCrime redeployment for Patrick (Phase 1). The choice model applies to the agents who can't be redeployed.

## The insight

Organisations default to telling people the decision is made and communicating the terms. The choice model inverts this: the company designs two outcomes, the employee selects one. The distinction is strategic, not sentimental. Employees who make a choice own the outcome. Employees who are told the outcome contest it.

## Evidence

- Contested redundancies cost multiples of the statutory amount in legal fees, management time, and leadership distraction
- Mutual separation rates are significantly lower in organisations that offer structured choice vs unilateral notice
- Employees who choose Path A (enhanced/faster) give you speed and discretion; those who choose Path B reveal their willingness to fight — before you've conceded anything
- Autonomy bias: people value having a choice even when the options are constrained. The subjective experience of "I chose this" vs "this was done to me" determines exit behaviour, Glassdoor reviews, and peer influence

## First Draft

### Employees who choose to leave cost less and litigate less than employees who are told to leave

Last month I had to right-size a small regional team. Automation had absorbed most of their workload. The business case was clear. The decision to reduce headcount was made. What wasn't decided was how.

The default script is well-worn: HR prepares a notice, legal reviews it, a manager delivers it. The company makes a decision and communicates it. The employee receives it. This is the standard approach to redundancy, and it has a problem — not a moral problem, a strategic one.

**When you give employees a genuine choice between two separation paths, they litigate less, leave faster, and cost you less than when you tell them the decision has already been made.**

Here's what I mean, and why it changes how I approach every restructuring now.

#### Contested redundancies are expensive, and most of the cost isn't the legal bill

The visible cost of a contested redundancy is the lawyer. The invisible cost is everything else: six months of management time, leadership attention pulled from growth work, team morale corroding as colleagues watch a drawn-out process, and the precedent it sets for everyone still employed.

Most people who contest redundancies aren't primarily motivated by the money. They're motivated by the feeling that something was done to them. That a decision was made, a process was run, and they were the object of it. The litigation is the expression of that feeling, not the cause.

People who made a choice — who were presented with two paths and selected one — don't carry that feeling. They might not like the outcome. But they own it. And people who own their outcome don't contest it.

The litigation rate on mutual separations, where both parties reach an agreed outcome, is a fraction of unilateral redundancy. This isn't a soft observation. It's a cost driver.

#### Choice doesn't slow the process — it accelerates it

The counterintuitive fear is that giving employees options creates more deliberation, more back-and-forth, a longer timeline. In practice, the opposite tends to be true.

A statutory process with appeal rights, grievance procedures, and legal review cycles can run for months. A structured choice conversation — here are two paths, here is what each looks like financially, you have one week to decide — closes in days.

The employees who are going to fight you will fight you regardless of which process you use. The choice model surfaces that faster and at lower cost, because they'll tell you by their selection which path they're choosing. An employee who picks the statutory minimum is signalling they're prepared for a longer process. An employee who picks the enhanced mutual route is telling you they want this closed. Either way, you know earlier.

#### The psychology is the point

There's a straightforward behavioural reason why this works, and it's worth naming directly.

Loss aversion and autonomy bias are powerful forces in how people experience difficult decisions. People value having a choice even when the options are constrained. An employee who selects "leave now, take the enhanced package" made a decision. One who receives "you're redundant, here is your notice" had something done to them. The financial outcome might be similar. The psychological experience is entirely different.

That difference matters after the person leaves. In how they talk about the company to former colleagues. In what they write online. In whether they refer candidates to you or steer them away. In small labour markets — and every specialist market is small — that signal travels.

The choice model doesn't just protect you legally. It protects the employer brand in communities where your next hire is probably connected to your last departure.

#### The framework: two paths, employee picks

The structure is simple enough to sketch in five minutes.

**Path A: Mutual separation.** Enhanced terms above statutory minimum. Faster close. Both parties sign off. The company pays more upfront for speed, discretion, and a clean exit.

**Path B: Statutory process.** Minimum legal entitlements. Full procedural timeline. The company follows the process to its conclusion.

Path A costs more in cash. Path B costs more in time, legal exposure, and relationship damage. Path A only applies if the employee chooses it — so you haven't pre-committed to enhanced terms, you've made them available conditional on a mutual agreement.

Employees who choose Path A give you speed and closure. Employees who choose Path B tell you exactly where you stand — and you still haven't given anything away, because the enhanced terms only attach on mutual acceptance.

One important caveat: the legal framing around this conversation matters by jurisdiction. "Without prejudice" protections vary significantly, particularly in markets where courts have treated informally framed separation conversations as binding admissions. Run the jurisdiction-specific check before you structure the offer. The framework is sound; the labelling needs local legal input.

#### What this changes about how you approach restructuring

Most People leaders I talk to still think about redundancy as a communication exercise. Draft the letter, schedule the meeting, deliver the news, process the exit. Clean and procedural.

The choice model reframes it as an offer design exercise. Before any conversation with the employee, you've designed both paths: the terms, the timeline, the financial outcome for each. You've run the legal check. You've set the decision window (one week is enough — "take the time you need" is an invitation to drag it out). You come to the conversation with a structure, not just a decision.

The difference is significant. You're not delivering news. You're presenting a decision framework and asking the employee to engage with it. That changes the dynamic of the room, the emotional register of the conversation, and the likelihood of a clean outcome.

**What to do about it:**

- Before your next redundancy, design both paths before any conversation with the employee. Have the numbers ready for each scenario.
- Run the jurisdiction-specific legal check on "without prejudice" framing. Don't assume it insulates you — verify the case law.
- Set a decision window. One week. Calendar it. "Take the time you need" extends unnecessarily and raises the temperature.
- Track your outcomes: mutual separation rate, time-to-exit, litigation rate. Build the data set. If you ever have to do this at scale, that evidence will matter to your CEO.
