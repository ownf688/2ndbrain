# Managers who meet but don't coach are the most dangerous kind

> **The take:** A manager with 50 one-on-ones and zero documented feedback isn't coaching -- they're performing management theatre. The meeting is the alibi.

## Context (private, not for publication)
First Manager Health Pulse at NALA (Jul 2, 2026). 16 managers tracked org-wide via Windmill. 12 of 16 had zero formal feedback given in 30 days despite active 1:1 calendars. One manager had 112 one-on-ones and zero feedback. The People Partner responsible for coaching other managers on this had also given zero feedback herself. Only 4 managers gave any feedback at all -- and 2 of those were Owen.

## The insight
Every company I've worked in tracks whether managers hold their 1:1s. Almost none track what comes out of them. The assumption is that meeting = coaching, but the data tells a different story.

When I ran the first org-wide audit of feedback vs meeting volume, the pattern was stark: managers with the highest 1:1 counts often had the lowest coaching output. They were meeting, not managing. The calendar looked healthy. The development pipeline was empty.

This matters because performance review cycles don't fail at review time -- they fail in the 6 months before, when no evidence is being collected. A manager who meets weekly but never documents a coaching observation, a developmental nudge, or a performance concern is building a blank scorecard. When review season arrives, they write from memory and vibes, not evidence. And memory is generous -- it smooths over the concerns they should have raised in real time.

The fix isn't more meetings. It's making the feedback artifact the unit of accountability, not the calendar event. Track whether managers are producing coaching outputs, not whether they're showing up to meetings.

## Evidence
- 12/16 managers: active 1:1 calendars, zero documented feedback in 30 days
- One manager: 112 one-on-ones, zero feedback entries
- Investigated whether 1:1 agenda edits could serve as a proxy -- they can't. Spot-checked content was operational task lists (budget approvals, project status), not coaching
- The performance review cycle that preceded this had the same symptom: managers writing from a blank page at review time

## First Draft

### Your Managers Are Meeting, Not Coaching. Here's How to Tell the Difference.

I tracked 16 managers across an entire company for 30 days. Every one of them had an active 1:1 calendar. Most met their reports weekly. One had 112 one-on-ones in a single month. Twelve of the sixteen had given zero documented feedback.

Not low feedback. Zero.

The calendar said "healthy management culture." The data said something else entirely.

**If your managers are holding 1:1s but producing no coaching artifacts, they're not managing. They're performing management theatre. The meeting is the alibi.**

#### 1:1 frequency is a vanity metric

Every People team I've worked in tracks 1:1 completion rates. It's the first thing on the manager health dashboard. It feels rigorous because it's measurable and binary: did the meeting happen, yes or no?

But it measures input, not output. A manager can hold 50 one-on-ones in a month and produce exactly nothing that develops their team. No feedback documented. No performance concern raised. No coaching observation written down. The meeting happened, so the dashboard stays green.

This is the equivalent of measuring a sales team by how many calls they made, not how much they closed. Activity is not impact.

The dangerous part is that 1:1 frequency creates a plausible story of health. When a senior leader asks "how are our managers doing?", the People team pulls the calendar data and says "95% 1:1 completion rate." Everyone nods. Nobody asks what happened in those meetings.

#### The blank scorecard problem

Performance reviews don't fail at review time. They fail in the six months before, when nobody is collecting evidence.

A manager who meets weekly but never documents a coaching observation, a developmental nudge, or a performance concern is building a blank scorecard. When review season arrives, they write from memory. And memory is generous. It smooths over the concerns they should have raised in real time. The difficult conversation they avoided in March becomes "met expectations" in September.

I've seen this play out in review cycles. Managers sit down to write assessments and have nothing to draw from. No notes. No documented feedback. No evidence trail. So they write from vibes, default to "meets expectations" for everyone, and the review cycle produces nothing useful.

The cost isn't just a bad review cycle. It's the six months of development that didn't happen. The performance concern that festered because nobody named it. The high performer who got the same rating as the person coasting beside them, and noticed.

#### The proxy trap: agenda edits are not coaching

When I found the 12-of-16 pattern, my first instinct was to look for proxy signals. Maybe the feedback was happening, just not in the formal feedback tool. Maybe it was in the 1:1 agenda itself.

So I audited the agenda content for the managers with the highest engagement scores. Some had hundreds of agenda edits per month. That looked promising. Until I read what they were actually writing.

Budget approvals. Project status updates. Litigation sign-offs. Probation deadline reminders. Recruitment pipeline reviews.

Every single agenda I checked was an operational task list, not a coaching conversation. The managers were using their 1:1s as status meetings. High engagement, zero development.

This taught me a rule I now apply to every People dashboard: the more an activity metric looks like it measures coaching, the more dangerous it is when it doesn't. Agenda edits look like preparation and engagement. Slack message volume looks like collaboration. Meeting count looks like cadence. Each one creates a plausible story that obscures the real question: is this manager developing their people?

#### What actually works: make the artifact the unit of accountability

The fix isn't more meetings, better agendas, or manager training about "coaching conversations." Those are all input-side interventions. The fix is output-side accountability.

Track whether managers are producing coaching artifacts. A coaching artifact is a documented observation about someone's performance, skills, or values, written close to the moment it happened. Not a vague "doing well" at review time. A specific "in Thursday's standup, you took ownership of the deployment incident and had a fix shipped by end of day -- that's exactly the kind of initiative I want to see more of."

When I shifted the manager health dashboard from calendar metrics to feedback artifact counts, the picture changed completely. The managers who looked healthy on 1:1 frequency suddenly showed up as the biggest gaps. And the few managers who were actually coaching (there were four, in a company of 250) became visible for the first time.

The dashboard now tracks:
- **Feedback artifacts given** in the last 30 days (the primary signal)
- **1:1 meetings held** (a hygiene check, not a quality signal)
- **Days since last documented coaching observation** per manager-report pair

Red means zero feedback and active 1:1s. Not zero 1:1s. Zero feedback despite 1:1s. That's the pattern that should worry you.

#### The second-order signal: who models it matters

One thing the data surfaced that I didn't expect: the person on my own team responsible for coaching other managers on feedback had given zero feedback herself in 30 days. She was running the adoption initiative while not adopting it.

Managers are pattern-matchers. They watch what the people holding them accountable actually do, not what they say to do. If the People team launches a feedback culture and the People Partners aren't visibly giving feedback, the initiative is dead before it starts.

Before you hold anyone accountable for a new standard, make sure your own team is the first and most visible example of it.

#### What to do about it

- **Pull your manager feedback data for the last 30 days.** Count how many managers gave zero documented feedback. If you don't have this data, that's your first problem.
- **Cross-reference against 1:1 calendars.** The dangerous pattern is high meeting count + zero feedback, not low meeting count. Managers who aren't meeting are visible. Managers who are meeting but not coaching are invisible.
- **Stop tracking 1:1 completion as a health metric.** Track coaching artifact output instead. The meeting is the container. The feedback is the content. Track the content.
- **Audit your own team first.** If your People Partners aren't giving feedback, fix that before you ask anyone else to.
