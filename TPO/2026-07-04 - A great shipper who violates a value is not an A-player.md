# A great shipper who violates a value is not an A-player

> **The take:** Values aren't a bonus dimension -- they're a veto. Poor in any single value means overall concern, regardless of delivery. Most companies say this. Almost none enforce it.

## Context (private, not for publication)
Built into NALA's EYS framework as a core principle, adapted from the QuantumLight/Revolut playbook. Three assessment dimensions (Deliverables, Skills, Values) scored independently. Values use yes/no behaviour statements ascending by level for all 4 NALA values (Customers First, Play to Win, Speed Wins, Understand Why). The first "No" fixes the level. Built full scorecards with 5 levels per value.

## The insight
Every company has values on the wall. Almost none have the mechanism to make values consequential. The reason is simple: it's easy to reward delivery because it's visible and measurable. It's hard to penalise values violations because the violator is often a high performer, and exiting a high performer feels like losing.

The structural fix is to make values a veto, not a dimension. When you score Deliverables, Skills, and Values independently, a top score on delivery with a poor score on values doesn't average out to "good enough." It flags as an overall concern. The person can ship, but they're corroding the culture that lets everyone else ship.

This requires three things most companies don't build:
1. **Behaviour-level scorecards for each value** -- not "lives our values (yes/no)" but ascending yes/no statements that define what each value looks like at each proficiency level
2. **Evidence accumulated in real time** -- not recalled at review time. Every meeting, every interaction is a chance to log a values signal.
3. **Executive commitment to act on the veto** -- meaning a top-performer exit when the values evidence demands it

Without all three, values remain aspirational. With all three, they become the thing that actually differentiates your culture.

## Evidence
- Built 4 value scorecards with 5 levels each (Poor through Exceptional), 12-13 behaviour statements per value
- Accumulating values evidence in real-time via meeting ingestion, not retroactively at review time
- The QuantumLight system at Revolut operates the same veto principle at 6,000+ employees
- Common failure mode: "brilliant jerk" tolerated because delivery numbers are strong, until the team around them hollows out

## First Draft

### Values Are a Veto, Not a Dimension. Here's How to Make Them Actually Bite.

Every company I've worked at had values on the wall. Posters in the office. A slide in the onboarding deck. A section in the performance review form that asked managers to tick a box: "Demonstrates company values: Yes / Sometimes / No."

None of it mattered. The highest performer who also happened to be the most corrosive person in the room got "Exceeds expectations" every cycle. Because they shipped. And shipping is visible, measurable, and hard to argue with.

I've watched this pattern hollow out teams. Not overnight. Slowly. The good people leave first, because they have options. The ones who stay learn that values are decorative. And the brilliant jerk at the centre of it all gets promoted, because the numbers look great.

**Values don't mean anything until they cost you something. Specifically: until they cost you a high performer. If a top scorer on delivery with a poor score on values still gets rated "strong," your values are wallpaper.**

#### Why values frameworks fail

Most companies assess values the same way they assess everything else: as one of several dimensions that get averaged together. Delivery is 40%, skills are 30%, values are 30%. A strong delivery score offsets a weak values score, and the person comes out "meets expectations" overall.

This is mathematically tidy and culturally disastrous. It means that someone who ships exceptionally but treats their colleagues badly, avoids accountability, or makes decisions without understanding why gets a passing grade. The average is fine. The signal is buried.

The problem gets worse in calibration. When a manager presents a direct report's scores, the conversation gravitates to delivery. "They shipped the migration on time, under budget." That's concrete. Measurable. Hard to argue with. The values concern -- "they threw a colleague under the bus in the post-mortem" -- is subjective, harder to quantify, and easier to dismiss.

So it gets dismissed. Review after review, cycle after cycle. The values section of the performance form becomes a formality. Managers tick the box. The system learns that values don't matter.

#### The structural fix: values as a veto

The fix isn't better training on values or more earnest all-hands presentations. It's a structural change to how values are scored and what happens when someone fails.

**Score values independently from delivery and skills.** Three dimensions, each assessed on its own:
1. **Deliverables** -- what they shipped, at what speed, quality, and complexity
2. **Skills** -- role-specific competencies (the same bar used in hiring)
3. **Values** -- behavioural evidence against each company value

When values are a separate dimension, they can't be averaged away. A top score on deliverables with a poor score on values doesn't balance out to "good enough." It flags as an overall concern. The person can ship, but they're corroding the environment that lets everyone else ship.

**Then make values a veto.** Poor in any single value means overall concern, regardless of performance on the other two dimensions. Not "something to watch." Not "development area." An overall concern that triggers a structured conversation about whether this person belongs.

This is the most controversial part, and the most important. It means you will occasionally have to look at someone who just delivered a brilliant quarter and say: "The delivery is excellent. The way you did it is not. And the way you did it matters as much as what you did."

#### You need scorecards, not checkboxes

The veto only works if you can defend it with evidence. "Doesn't live our values" is an opinion. "Responded to direct feedback with defensiveness or explanation rather than correction -- that's a Poor-level behaviour on our Play to Win scorecard" is a fact against a defined standard.

Here's what I mean by behaviour-level scorecards. For each company value, define ascending yes/no behaviour statements across five levels: Poor, Basic, Intermediate, Advanced, Exceptional. The first "No" fixes the level.

Example for a value like "Play to Win":

| Level | Behaviour |
|-------|-----------|
| Poor | Defers decisions, avoids accountability, passes problems sideways |
| Poor | Responds to direct feedback with defensiveness rather than correction |
| Basic | Takes ownership of outcomes, not just effort. Drives tasks to completion |
| Basic | Receives feedback calmly and acts on it |
| Intermediate | Holds peers accountable, not just direct reports |
| Intermediate | Makes hard calls: exits underperformers, kills failing projects |
| Advanced | Builds accountability into systems, not just personal discipline |
| Exceptional | Their presence raises the bar for everyone around them |

Read from the top. If the person hits a "No" at the Poor level, they're Poor on this value. It doesn't matter how many Advanced behaviours they also show. The veto fires.

This takes work to build. Four values, five levels each, 12-13 behaviour statements per value. But it converts "doesn't live our values" from a vibes-based judgment into a documented, defensible, calibratable assessment.

#### Evidence in real time, not at review time

The second piece most companies miss: evidence collection.

If you only look for values evidence at review time, you'll find nothing. Memory is selective. Managers remember deliverables because they're tied to project milestones and deadlines. Values signals are tied to moments -- a comment in a meeting, a reaction to feedback, a decision made under pressure. Those moments disappear from memory within weeks.

The fix is to accumulate values evidence continuously. Every meeting, every 1:1, every project retro is an opportunity to note a values signal -- positive or negative. "In Thursday's standup, they took ownership of the deployment incident without being asked" (Play to Win, positive). "In the post-mortem, they blamed the upstream team instead of investigating their own contribution" (Understand Why, watch).

Over a quarter, these observations accumulate into a rich evidence base. When review time arrives, the manager isn't writing from a blank page. They're curating from months of documented signals. The review becomes a summary of what already happened, not a creative writing exercise.

This is where most values frameworks die. Not at the design stage -- the scorecards are easy to build. Not at the policy stage -- leadership will happily sign off on "values matter." They die in the daily discipline of actually noticing and recording values behaviour between review cycles.

#### The CEO commitment test

The third piece is the hardest. The veto means nothing if leadership won't act on it.

At some point, you will have a high performer who scores Exceptional on deliverables and Poor on a value. The numbers will argue for keeping them. Their manager will argue for keeping them. The team might even argue for keeping them, because they're good at the technical work.

And you'll have to say: "The values veto fired. Here's the evidence. We need to have a structured conversation about whether this person's impact on the culture outweighs their impact on delivery."

If the CEO isn't willing to have that conversation -- and potentially lose a high performer -- then the veto is theatre. The rest of the organisation will learn that it doesn't apply to people who ship, and the values will go back to being wallpaper.

This is why values-as-a-veto has to be a leadership commitment, not an HR policy. The Head of People can build the scorecards, set up the evidence collection, and design the assessment process. But the moment of truth is when a senior leader looks at the evidence and decides to act. That decision has to come from the top.

#### What to do about it

- **Build behaviour-level scorecards for each value.** Not "lives our values (yes/no)" -- ascending yes/no statements at five proficiency levels. First "No" fixes the level.
- **Score values independently from delivery and skills.** Three dimensions, each assessed separately. No averaging. A poor values score cannot be offset by strong delivery.
- **Start collecting evidence now.** Don't wait for the next review cycle. Log values signals -- positive and negative -- as they happen, in whatever system your managers use for 1:1 notes or feedback.
- **Get your CEO to commit to the veto in advance.** Before the next calibration, agree: if someone scores Poor on any value, it's an overall concern regardless of delivery. If leadership won't commit to this principle, the rest is academic.
