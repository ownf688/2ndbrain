## Key Discussion Points

### Compliance and documentation strength

Kelvin's strongest area. He has direct experience managing regulatory deadlines, document completeness, and stakeholder coordination from his banking background. He frames his approach around proactive buffer timelines rather than reactive deadline-chasing.

> [!quote]- Source
> "I'll also come up with a tracker that has a buffer time. Instead of waiting until the due date, we have enough time to get the documents in order. And get them signed and get them to the regulator in time. This will enable us to not be reacting to it but to already have everything in place instead of just waiting."

### Version control and document management (weakest answer)

When asked how he would keep a licensing filing folder audit-ready, version-controlled, and easy to find, Kelvin gave a vague answer about keeping "all the versions" and having a backup. When Lynnette pressed for specifics on tools and structure, he defaulted to "in my folders or files." No naming conventions, no document management system, no audit trail methodology.

> [!quote]- Source
> "I plan on making sure I have all the versions ready, like the initial version, the second version, and the final version. I make sure I have all the versions with me."
>
> Lynnette: "Where?"
>
> "Um, let's say in my, in my folders or files, so that in case even if the final version is I can trace back and know that this is the second one, the second last version."

### Risk-based prioritization under pressure

Solid real-world example from banking: faced with competing compliance and risk document deadlines affecting a key customer, he triaged by risk exposure and business impact, submitted risk documents first, then negotiated a 24-hour extension with compliance for the remainder. Contacted 17 customers for supporting documents within the extended window.

> [!quote]- Source
> "So I went for the risky parts, the risky documents first, ensuring that I had the correct documents and I arrived on time, then checking up with the deadline in that sequence. I look at the risk part of it first, then the deadline and the business impact."

> [!quote]- Source (on requesting the extension)
> "I was not able to completely submit documents to compliance, but I immediately contacted them and requested for an extra 24 hours because I had to engage around 17 customers for supporting documents on the incoming funds."

### Escalation approach

Shows a structured escalation path: verify the communication was received, try alternative channels (call if email failed), identify blockers collaboratively, then escalate to his manager with full context of steps taken and risk exposure. Sound instincts, though described generically rather than with a sharp real example.

> [!quote]- Source
> "If they have not gotten the communication or they have not understood what I was looking for, I'll go ahead and explain it to them. And if they have received the information and have not really responded to it, I look, I maybe try alternative means before escalation. Maybe if I have sent an email, I do a call and just request getting to know if there are any blockers."

### Internal deadline discipline

His strongest conceptual contribution: he sets personal deadlines ahead of regulatory deadlines to preserve options. This is the right instinct for an ops role, though he didn't describe how he systematizes it beyond a personal Excel tracker.

> [!quote]- Source
> "What would make me raise my hand early and escalate things is I already set an internal deadline, and if that deadline comes overdue, then I raise that. Because when you wait, you run out of options. When you have enough time, then you have multiple options."

### Multi-jurisdiction is entirely new

Kelvin explicitly flags multi-jurisdiction work as his biggest learning area. His experience is single-market (Kenyan banking). He frames it as an upside ("excited about") but it is a genuine gap for a role that requires navigating regulatory frameworks across multiple African and European markets.

> [!quote]- Source
> "What I feel is new to this role and which I'm very excited about is the multi-jurisdiction part of it, and I'm sure I'll be learning more and even growing with the role."

### Tooling and systems maturity

Google Sheets, Excel, Jira (learning), and an internal CRM built by a previous employer's IT team. No experience with modern compliance/document management platforms, workflow automation, or the kind of tooling a scaling fintech uses. He acknowledged the traditional-to-fintech gap himself.

> [!quote]- Source
> "I'm coming from a traditional banking part of it and I'm trying to enter into the fintech market where things are fast moving and there's a lot of new regulations, new goodness here and there."

### Career gap and motivation

Left Family Bank in 2025. Describes it as a deliberate career reset. Attracted to NALA for the cross-border payments mission and African market focus. Says salary is not his only motivation. Comfortable with the 80K-100K KES band. Available to start immediately.

> [!quote]- Source
> "For me, I took a deliberate step back. I wanted to be more concise about the next chapter I wanted to add into."

> [!quote]- Source
> "Salary is not my only motivation. I'm also looking at the impact I'll be making within the organization and also the learning opportunities that come with the role."

### Current team context (from Lynnette)

Entity operations in Kenya currently has one person, working closely with compliance and vendor onboarding. NALA is scaling fast, doubling vendor onboarding volume. The hire would support that existing team member.

> [!quote]- Source (Lynnette)
> "The current pain points as of now is there is very high demand and the job scale is very cumbersome for just one person."
